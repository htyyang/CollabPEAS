CSCW Companion ’26, October 10–14, 2026, Salt Lake City, UT, USA 1 Introduction
Artificial Intelligence (AI) agents perceive their environment through
sensors and act through actuators in pursuit of goals. They are now
embedded in everyday systems [16], from voice assistants and rec-
ommendation systems to autonomous navigation [8]. Although
people may interact with these systems regularly, the design de-
cisions behind the agent often remain hidden. This gap is central
to AI literacy education, where learning AI requires more than
learning how to use AI tools. Learners also need to understand
how agents sense, decide, act, and how their behavior is shaped by
human-authored specifications [11]. However, for most learners,
the process of specifying an AI agent remains opaque.
The PEAS framework offers a way to make this hidden specifi-
cation process more explicit. PEAS structures agent design around
four interdependent components: Performance measure, Environ-
ment, Actuators, and Sensors [16]. By asking learners to define what
an agent should achieve, where it operates, how it can act, and what
information it can sense, PEAS turns the behavior of the agent into
a set of design choices that can be examined and discussed. In doing
so, it provides a useful scaffold for translating professional agent
design reasoning into learning-oriented activities.
Using this scaffold in a learning activity also requires attention to
how learners engage with AI concepts. Research on AI literacy edu-
cation points to the value of collaborative learning, as peer explana-
tion can help learners remain engaged with abstract concepts [11].
This focus on collaboration aligns with agent specification, which
often requires people to negotiate task definitions, weigh sensor and
actuator trade-offs, and interpret emergent behavior [16]. These
considerations motivate our system, which frames the specification
of PEAS-based agents as a collaborative activity. Immersive Virtual
Reality (VR) is well-suited for this activity, as it supports spatial
co-presence, allows partners to jointly observe and discuss agent
behavior [6], and grounds abstract PEAS components as visible,
manipulable objects [7, 10], making sensing, acting, and environ-
mental constraints directly observable in ways that are difficult to
support through text-based or 2D interfaces [11].
We present CollabPEAS, a collaborative VR system for AI literacy
education in which learner dyads specify a virtual cleaning robot
using the PEAS framework and block-based coding, coming to un-
derstand how agent behavior is produced by human specification.
The system integrates three design elements: a VR environment
that makes PEAS components spatially grounded and observable, a
block-based coding interface that preserves agent design reasoning
while reducing the burden of programming syntax, and collabora-
tion supports designed to scaffold productive dyadic specification
work.
2 Related Work
2.1 AI Literacy and the PEAS Framework
AI literacy refers to the competencies that enable people to criti-
cally evaluate AI technologies, collaborate with AI, and use AI in
daily life and work. These competencies are essential for informed
participation in an AI-mediated world. Long and Magerko [11]
provide a comprehensive framework that identifies seventeen com-
petencies and fifteen design considerations; CollabPEAS specifically
targets five of these: Recognizing AI (C1), Decision-Making (C8),
Wanru (Jovie) Li, Yichen (Andy) Yu, and Qiao (Georgie) Jin
Action and Reaction (C14), Sensors (C15), and Programmability
(C17). Existing AI literacy interventions for non-technical learn-
ers have used media-rich curricula, hands-on machine learning
activities, and block-based environments such as Scratch [14] and
related tools [18], helping learners understand what AI is and how
it operates at a conceptual level.
However, a remaining challenge is the lack of designs that let
learners practice the structured specification skills used to design
AI agents: existing approaches primarily teach about AI agents,
with fewer opportunities to reason through what an agent should
sense, how it should act, and how success should be evaluated.
In response, we use PEAS as a guiding scaffold for learner-facing
agent design. PEAS [16] decomposes agent design into four compo-
nents: Performance measure, Environment, Actuators, and Sensors.
Although PEAS is already used as a teaching scaffold in introduc-
tory AI education, our contribution is to adapt it into an embodied
collaborative experience that makes each component a target for
AI-literacy competency development.
2.2 Collaborative Learning in Immersive VR
Collaborative learning can effectively support AI literacy compe-
tencies. As Long and Magerko [11] note, collaborative activities
can sustain engagement and promote conceptual understanding
through peer explanation. Previous work has explored different
collaboration strategies, such as role-based collaboration [17] and
flexible adaptive collaboration modes [20], to support AI literacy
and related computational competencies such as programming.
In this work, we use immersive virtual reality (VR) to support
collaborative learning of AI literacy in AI agent design. VR pro-
vides a shared spatial environment where learners can observe
agent behaviors, discuss design choices, and connect AI concepts
with embodied interaction. Previous research on collaborative VR
found that VR can improve knowledge acquisition, social presence,
and collaboration quality [6]. Interactive VR environments can in-
crease the germane cognitive load when embodied interactions are
conceptually meaningful rather than decorative [5]. For AI agent
specification, VR is especially suitable because PEAS components
are spatial and physical: sensors perceive, actuators act, and envi-
ronments constrain. VR makes these components observable and
jointly inspectable, transforming the specification into an embod-
ied social activity where partners can see, discuss, and manipulate
shared agent components [6, 7, 10]. This aligns with the theory of
embodied interaction, which argues that spatial engagement with
a system can shape how learners reason about it [3].
3 Design Goals
CollabPEAS is designed to help non-technical learners understand
AI agents as systems whose behavior results from connected choices
about performance measures, environment, sensors, actuators, and
decision rules. The three design goals below (DG1–DG3) state what
learners must achieve; Sections 4.1 and 4.2 describe how each is
performed.
•Make the PEAS framework observable and consequen-
tial(DG1). Existing AI literacy interventions often teach
about AI agents conceptually, but rarely let learners see
how specification decisions produce agent behaviors [11].

Because PEAS links performance measures, environments,
actuators, and sensors with resulting behavior [16], learners
need to see the consequences of their choices, not only their
labels. We therefore set DG1: every specification decision
must be immediately observable in the robot’s behavior.
•Support learning through progressive scaffolding(DG2).
PEAS involves four interdependent components that must
be understood together, but presenting them all at once can
overwhelm non-technical learners [11]. Structured progres-
sions in block-based environments support computational
thinking without programming prerequisites [14, 19]. For
example, learners should grasp why a performance measure
matters before choosing sensors to achieve it, not encoun-
tering all four components at once. We therefore set DG2:
learners must build their specification one component at a
time, preserving how each constrains the next.
•Collaborative scaffold agent design(DG3). The speci-
fication of AI agents is collaborative in practice [16], and
collaboration matters for the learning of AI literacy [11].
However, unstructured collaboration can be less productive
for novices [2, 17], while fixed roles can limit shared own-
ership [20]. For example, if either partner could unilaterally
finalize a sensor choice, one learner could dominate while
the other disengages. We therefore set DG3: partners must
negotiate and jointly own each decision, not work in parallel
or under fixed roles.
4 System Design
CollabPEAS is a multiplayer VR learning environment built in
Unity 6 for Meta Quest headsets, using Photon Fusion 2 with Shared
Authority topology for real-time synchronization. Both participants
occupy the same virtual space with access to all shared objects. The
system has two main components: the PEAS-Guided Design Flow
(Section 4.1), which addresses DG1 and DG2, and the Collaboration
Design Supports (Section 4.2), which address DG3.
4.1 PEAS-Guided Design Flow
The learning experience to realize DG1 (observability) and DG2
(progressive scaffolding) is a five-step design flow in which dyads
collaboratively specify a household cleaning robot, tying each PEAS
choice to observable behavior (DG1) while introducing components
incrementally rather than all at once (DG2).
•Define purpose and performance measures. Participants
identify what their robot is for and how to evaluate its suc-
cess, distinguishing a goal (“clean the apartment”) from a
performance measure (“percent of floor area cleared in 10
minutes”) that determines how the agent behaves.
•Characterize environment and select sensors. Partic-
ipants walk through the 3D apartment and select which
sensors (camera, distance, touch, etc.) to mount on their ro-
bot; different sensor choices yield different robot behaviors
in the same environment, making perception and capability
directly observable (DG1).
•Choose actuators. Participants select the physical capabili-
ties (wheels, arms, cleaning tools) that define what the robot
can do, connecting each selection back to the performance
measure: if the measure requires a behavior, the actuators
must be capable of producing it.
•Program agent behavior through block-based coding.
Participants specify the robot’s decision logic by arranging
pre-coded draggable 3D block cards into four-slot chains:
SENSE →CONDITION →ACTION →CONTROL. The
system enforces compatibility between blocks: a Distance
Sensor block returns NEAR, FAR, or BLOCKED, so only an
obstacle condition such as IF obstacle NEAR can snap into
Slot 2, while a condition reading a different sensor’s value,
such as IF floor DIRTY, bounces back with a brief label. Each
completed chain is a discrete decision rule; because all rules
run in parallel and whichever sensor fires first on a given tick
triggers its rule’s action, the robot’s behavior emerges from
the environment rather than a fixed script. No programming
knowledge is required [9], which operationalizes DG2 by
letting non-technical learners engage directly in decision-
rule construction.
•Test, debug and revision; Participants observe their robot
running in the apartment and identify mismatches between
the intended and observed behavior. The debug loop is scaf-
folded as design reasoning rather than error correction: the
productive question is not “what is broken?” but “is the agent
rational given what we have specified, and if not, which
PEAS component needs to change?”
4.2 Collaboration Design Supports
To address DG3 (scaffolded collaboration), the system offers five col-
laboration supports for how partners coordinate within the PEAS
workflow, communicating throughout via in-VR voice chat. The
supports occupy a middle ground between fully symmetric access
and fully role-assigned collaboration, drawing on evidence that
unstructured collaboration can be less effective than structured
alternatives [17] and that shared control with awareness tools im-
proves collaboration quality in VR [6].
•Agree-before-placing. When one participant drags a block
toward a placement slot, it enters a pending state visible to
both; the second participant must approve, request discus-
sion, or suggest an alternative before the placement takes
effect. The mechanism keeps every specification decision ex-
plicitly agreed on, creating a window for verbal negotiation.
•Alternating lead-reviewer roles. Each design step assigns
one participant as lead (initiating placements) and the other
as reviewer (approving, rejecting, or suggesting changes),
with roles alternating each step so that both experience both
perspectives. This balances evidence that role-based struc-
ture improves learning outcomes [17] with preserving shared
ownership, since alternating assignments avoid permanently
excluding either participant.
•Structured inter-step reflection prompts. At each transi-
tion between steps, the system pauses and presents a joint
reflection question both participants must answer before
proceeding, e.g., after sensor selection: “Do you both agree
these sensors give the robot enough information to do its
job?” These prompts aim to create checkpoints for alignment
and connect each new decision back to those already made.

•Dual observation logging. During Test, debug, and revise,

then highlights agreement or divergence, creating discussion
points for debugging. The goal is to turn debugging from a
single-perspective activity into a collaborative comparison
of interpretations [12].
These supports aim to structure the joint specification enough
to prevent coordination failures associated with fully symmetric
access [17], while preserving the shared ownership that defines
collaborative problem solving [15]. Such a structure can also intro-
duce friction, slow exploration, or add pressure to negotiate every
move; we treat the right level of structure as an open question our
planned evaluation will examine, including whether some supports
should be loosened as partners become more familiar with the task.
5 Conclusion and Future Work
CollabPEAS is a collaborative AI-literacy system currently under
active development. The PEAS-guided design flow, the block-based
coding interface, and the VR environment are implemented in Unity
6 with Photon Fusion 2 networking, and the five collaboration
supports are fully specified.
Once these supports are implemented, we plan to evaluate the
complete system through a mixed-methods between-subject study
with non-technical participants recruited on campus, testing in Au-
gust, comparing the VR condition against a functionally equivalent
2D desktop interface. This comparison is designed in part to assess
whether the learning advantages persist beyond the novelty effects
documented in VR learning research [4]. The evaluation will assess
the AI literacy outcomes through pre/post knowledge assessments
aligned with the targeted competencies of [11], attitudinal changes
through an adapted AI Literacy Questionnaire [13], and collabora-
tive dynamics through behavioral logs, mid-session collaboration
checks and semi-structured interviews analyzed through thematic
analysis [1].
Within this evaluation, a central focus is whether the collab-
oration supports produce more equitable participation and more
verbal negotiation than would emerge from fully symmetric access
alone, and whether this, in turn, is associated with a deeper concep-
tual understanding of how the PEAS components relate to agent
behavior [17, 20]. Whether this expectation holds, and how the
five supports compare to one another remain open empirical ques-
tions. We hope that the findings will be useful to researchers and
designers in the Computer-Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW)
community who work on collaborative tools for technical learning.
